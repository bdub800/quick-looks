## Section 2 - The business model: how they actually make money

Lululemon is a vertical premium athleisure brand. It designs its own products, manufactures through a network of third-party vendors concentrated in Vietnam, Bangladesh, and Cambodia, and sells primarily through company-operated stores and its own e-commerce channel. Wholesale exposure is minimal by design - the company captures the retail mark-up internally, which is the core mechanism behind its industry-leading gross margins.

The model has three layers. First, a fabric and product IP franchise: proprietary fabrics (Luon, Nulu, Everlux, and the Align and Wunder legging platforms built on them) justify the $90-$140 price point for core women's bottoms and create switching costs for loyal customers who associate the feel of the fabric with the Lululemon shopping experience. The FY2025 10-K describes product innovation as the foundational competitive moat.

Second, a direct channel that keeps pricing full. Company-operated stores generated approximately 45% of FY2021 revenue; e-commerce (DTC) generated another 44%. By FY2025, the channel breakdown had shifted slightly toward stores as the international store fleet expanded, with e-commerce growing +8% YoY. The absence of meaningful wholesale means Lululemon does not take haircuts from third-party retailers and does not compete against itself at marked-down prices. This is where the 56-59% gross margin comes from: charging $120 for leggings, pocketing the retail margin directly.

Third, an international buildout, particularly in China Mainland, that is now the company's primary growth engine. In FY2025, Lululemon opened 21 net new stores in China Mainland, with China comparable sales running at +20%. The company added six new markets in December 2025 through franchise agreements - Greece, Austria, Poland, Hungary, Romania, and India - using a capital-light model for earlier-stage geographies. The company also acquired its Mexico operations directly in September 2024.

The model earns its margin from brand positioning at the top of the athleisure price spectrum, a vertically integrated channel that avoids wholesale discounting, and a product innovation cycle built on fabric platforms that are not easily replicated. The tariff regime and the Americas demand softness both hit that model at its most load-bearing points: cost structure and [home-market pricing power](https://eightx.co/blog/apparel-cpi-vs-dtc-pricing-power-2026).

## Section 3 - Strengths: the moat that is real

**1. A gross margin structure that holds at 56-59% despite a major cost shock.** Lululemon's gross margin peaked at 59.2% in FY2024 and fell to 56.6% in FY2025 under a quantified $275M tariff hit. Even at 54.2% in Q1 FY2026 - the worst margin quarter in years - Lululemon is running structurally above where Nike, Adidas, and Under Armour typically land on their apparel segments. That gap is the premium pricing model working: $120 leggings sold through a company-owned channel with no wholesale markup surrendered produces a different margin profile than broadline sportswear distributed through Dick's and Foot Locker. The gross margin compression is real; the structural premium over peers remains intact. Source: LULU 10-K FY2024 and FY2025.

**2. A fortress balance sheet that funds the capital cycle and buybacks simultaneously.** As of February 1, 2026 (FY2025 year-end), Lululemon held $1.81B in cash, zero financial debt, and a $600M revolving credit facility that was fully undrawn. Over the five fiscal years FY2021-FY2025, the company repurchased approximately $4.6B of its own stock, reducing diluted share count from approximately 125M (FY2020) to 111.4M at FY2025 year-end and 109.3M by Q1 FY2026. In FY2025 alone, the company repurchased $1.18B of shares while absorbing $275M of tariff cost. That is a capital return and balance sheet profile that gives management genuine optionality during a margin trough. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025, Notes to Financial Statements; balance sheet selected data.

**3. China Mainland as a structurally high-growth international engine.** China revenue grew from $577M in FY2022 to $1.75B in FY2025 - a 3-year CAGR of approximately 45% - on comparable sales of +67% (FY2023), +25% (FY2024, +27% cc), +20% (FY2025, +19% cc). (Note: FY2024 China segment revenue grew +41%, reflecting both comp growth and net new store openings; the comparable-sales figure was +25% (+27% constant currency).) The FY2025 10-K discloses 21 net new stores opened in China Mainland in FY2025, with comparable sales +20%. Q1 FY2026 China printed +30% reported (+23% constant currency) per the Q1 FY2026 press release. That is not a promotional bump or a channel-fill; it is brand velocity in a market where the premium athleisure category is genuinely underpenetrated relative to Western markets. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025; LULU Q1 FY2026 press release.

**4. A product and innovation franchise with demonstrated pricing power over time.** The Align legging, the Wunder Train, the ABC pant and the Scuba hoodie are not just SKUs - they are the brand's durable platforms, each with dedicated followings and multi-year repurchase cycles. The FY2025 10-K notes that Women's grew +5% and Men's grew +4% for the full year, with Accessories +8%. The brand's vertical channel, in-store educator model, and community-driven ambassador program have historically produced organic demand generation that requires limited traditional advertising spend. SG&A running at 36.6% of FY2025 revenue is not low, but a meaningful portion is store operations (real estate, labor) rather than paid advertising. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025 MD&A.

## Section 4 - Weaknesses: the cracks in the 10-Q

**1. Americas comparable sales have deteriorated for two consecutive years - and the FY2025 10-K language confirms this is structural, not assortment.** Americas comps went from +14% (FY2022) to -1% (FY2023) to -1% (FY2024) to -3% (FY2025) and remained negative in Q1 FY2026 (Americas comp -5%, or -6% constant currency; total company was -2% constant currency as International ran +13%). The FY2025 10-K explicitly states: "lower conversion rates, store traffic, and average order value" - all three demand drivers moving in the wrong direction simultaneously. In FY2023, management characterized the issue as an assortment problem. By FY2025, the language evolved: the action plan addresses product innovation, in-store experience AND digital storytelling, signaling that the company no longer believes assortment alone explains the shortfall. Americas product margin also fell -340 basis points in FY2025, combining tariff impact with markdown pressure. First disclosed explicitly in the FY2023 10-K; reached headline status in FY2025. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025 MD&A; FY2023-FY2025 risk-factor timeline.

**2. The tariff shock was underestimated in real time and is ongoing.** The FY2023 10-K (filed March 2024) stated that the company "does not expect current changes to have a material impact on the cost of our products." Twelve months later, the FY2025 10-K quantified an "approximately $275 million" unmitigated gross profit reduction from US tariffs and de minimis removal, with "continued decline in our gross margin and operating margin in 2026" explicitly guided. Lululemon's sourcing concentrates in Vietnam, Bangladesh, and Cambodia - all subject to elevated IEEPA tariff rates. Its US e-commerce is historically fulfilled from Canadian distribution centers, which historically qualified for de minimis exemption. The August 29, 2025 de minimis removal hit both exposures simultaneously. The company remitted $216M in IEEPA tariffs in FY2025 whose refund is uncertain. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025 (tariff section); 10-K FY2023 (prior disclosure).

**3. Competitive intensity from Alo Yoga and Vuori is capping Lululemon's ability to price up to offset tariff costs.** The 10-K FY2025 risk factor states: "Our brand positioning, products, and marketing efforts may not be considered distinct, culturally relevant, or desirable to guests." That language is the filing-level acknowledgment of a brand-heat risk that industry commentary attributes specifically to Alo Yoga (fashion-forward, celebrity-adjacent, pricing in the same premium tier) and Vuori (men's lifestyle, backed by $400M of SoftBank capital and planning 100+ US stores). The prescriptions bulls often reach for - "just raise prices to pass through tariffs" - fail the realism check here: Lululemon already sits at the top of the premium athleisure price spectrum alongside Alo and Vuori. The FY2025 10-K's own Americas action plan names selective price increases as one lever but explicitly acknowledges mitigation "is not expected to fully offset" tariff costs. Pricing power is real but bounded. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025 Item 1A; published analyst commentary, 2026.

**4. China growth is covering for Americas weakness - which means LULU is now more exposed to China risk than it has ever been.** China Mainland grew from 7.1% of FY2022 revenue to 15.8% of FY2025 revenue. Every quarter China grows faster than Americas shrinks, total revenue holds up - which is exactly what happened in FY2025 (+5% total despite Americas -1%). But that coverage increases China concentration: if China softens for any reason - US-China geopolitical escalation, anti-Western consumer nationalism, a domestic economic slowdown, or regulatory intervention - the structural offset that is currently holding total revenue together disappears. The FY2023 10-K was the first filing to explicitly flag "geopolitical instability" in China as a material risk factor. By FY2025, China was 15.8% of revenue and the risk factor language had expanded to cover US-China trade tension bilaterally. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025 Item 1A; LULU 10-K FY2023 Item 1A.

## Section 5 - Opportunities and threats

The opportunity slate is real, though each item comes with a structural caveat.

International expansion beyond China is the clearest incremental growth lever. In December 2025, Lululemon announced franchise-based market entries in Greece, Austria, Poland, Hungary, Romania and India for 2026 - six new markets using a capital-light model that allows faster geographic reach without the balance sheet load of company-operated stores. Rest of World revenue grew +16% in FY2025 and +19% in FY2024; the franchise layer accelerates this with lower capital commitment.

Men's remains structurally underpenetrated. Men's revenue grew +4% in FY2025 on a base that is still a fraction of women's. Vuori's aggressive US expansion - backed by SoftBank capital - is specifically targeting the same male premium lifestyle consumer Lululemon has been developing since 2014. The threat is real, but so is the runway: men's penetration at Lululemon remains below category share in the broader activewear market.

Footwear is a potential margin-enhancing expansion. Lululemon's Beyondfeel running platform is in early commercial rollout. Footwear at $100-$130 per pair carries margin profiles similar to apparel and extends the brand into a category where On Running (public, growing fast) and Hoka (Deckers) are expanding into apparel - making the category adjacency a two-way contest.

The threats are weighted toward the current reality, not speculation. Tariff persistence is the most quantifiable: the company itself has guided to "continued adverse effect" in FY2026. Supply chain restructuring away from Vietnam/Bangladesh/Cambodia takes 18-30 months, per industry supply-chain analysis - well beyond any near-term resolution. Americas saturation is structural if traffic, conversion, and AOV are all declining simultaneously. Alo Yoga and Vuori are not a "risk that might emerge" - they are the brands the FY2025 risk-factor language is pointing at without naming. And a China geopolitical disruption - whether US-imposed sanctions, Chinese consumer nationalism directed at US brands, or a domestic Chinese economic shock - would remove the growth engine that is currently holding total revenue together.

## Section 6 - The macro environment

Lululemon is flying through four distinct macro forces, and two of them are pointing in the same bad direction simultaneously.

The US discretionary apparel environment is soft for premium brands. Consumer confidence and spending in the US have been under pressure from elevated interest rates and residual inflation in non-discretionary spending categories. The 10-K FY2025 explicitly identifies "lower conversion rates, store traffic, and average order value" in the Americas, which is not a macro summary - it is what soft discretionary consumer spending looks like on a brand's own analytics. For a brand priced at the top of the athleisure tier, the first budget casualty in a consumer-pressure environment is the $130 legging rather than the entry-level alternative.

The tariff regime is the most acute macro force and the most brand-specific in its impact. Lululemon's sourcing concentration in Vietnam, Bangladesh, and Cambodia creates direct duty exposure on the cost side. Its US DTC e-commerce fulfillment from Canadian distribution centers created de minimis exposure on the revenue side. Both risks materialized in FY2025. The critical distinction from competitors like Nike and Under Armour: those companies run diversified global supply chains across more geographies, with existing manufacturing in regions less affected by IEEPA rates. Lululemon's sourcing concentration, historically a cost-efficiency advantage, became a tariff concentration risk the moment the IEEPA regime engaged.

The Chinese consumer is the macro tailwind that is currently doing the heaviest lifting. China's premium lifestyle category has secular growth characteristics that resemble what the US athleisure market looked like in 2015-2018: a nascent but fast-expanding consumer class trading up to premium Western brands as a quality and status signal. Lululemon's China comparable sales +20% in FY2025 and +13% constant currency in Q1 FY2026 are operating in a structurally different demand environment than the Americas. The risk is that China macro conditions (property sector stress, youth unemployment, export-led trade tensions with the US) compress that consumer confidence faster than the store buildout can absorb.

The athleisure category itself is maturing in the US. The 2015-2023 expansion of premium athleisure was driven by a structural shift in consumer dress norms (athleisure as everyday wear, not just workout wear) and by a first-mover brand - Lululemon - that had years of unchallenged premium positioning. That window has closed. The category in the US is now described by analysts as "saturated," and Lululemon's 21.2% share of US athleisure spending faces direct pressure from Alo Yoga at similar price points, Vuori in men's, and value alternatives at lower price points. The tariff regime compounds this by putting pressure on Lululemon to raise prices in a market that already has full-priced premium alternatives.

## Section 7 - The CFO verdict and the operator bridge

The Street's bull/bear debate on LULU in 2026 reduces to a single question: is the Q1 FY2026 operating margin of 11.2% transitory or structural? Bulls (Bernstein at $220, Seeking Alpha's contrarian camp at $229-$260) argue that the 410-basis-point Q1 gross margin decline is overwhelmingly tariff- and product-cycle-driven, rolls off by FY2027 as sourcing migrates and the new CEO (Heidi O'Neill, starting September 8, 2026) resets the product cadence, and that a 10x trailing earnings multiple on a debt-free compounder with real brand equity is pricing in no recovery at all. Bears (Jefferies at $120, Wells Fargo downgrading June 5, 2026) argue that this is an operating-cost breakdown - revenue decelerating while SG&A grows - and that the "disappointing product launches" language in the Q1 FY2026 press release is the company itself confirming that the FY2025 SKU proliferation strategy failed.

My read: the bulls are right about the balance sheet and wrong about the timeline. The bears are right about the structural nature of Americas weakness but wrong to price the stock as if the franchise value is impaired. The differentiated Eightx thesis is this: the real risk is not tariffs, which are a cost shock with a disclosed dollar amount and a multi-year mitigation path. The real risk is the intersection of three forces that the 10-K arc shows converging over four years: Americas demand is structurally softer as premium athleisure saturates and Alo/Vuori take share; the tariff regime amplifies that softness by raising prices in the market where consumers are already showing price sensitivity; and China growth is covering for both - which means LULU is increasingly betting a 15.8% revenue concentration on a single foreign market in a geopolitical environment that is explicitly unfavorable.

"Just raise prices to offset tariffs" fails the realism check directly. Alo Yoga competes at the same price point. Vuori is growing men's faster than Lululemon with SoftBank-funded retail expansion. The FY2025 action plan names "selective price increases" as one lever - not a primary one - and the company's own disclosures state that mitigation "is not expected to fully offset" the tariff cost. The lever that is actually available and already mid-execution is sourcing diversification: moving production volume away from Vietnam/Bangladesh/Cambodia toward lower-duty geographies. That is an 18-30 month runway, not a next-quarter fix.

The operator bridge. A $5-80M premium apparel or consumer brand very likely has a version of the Lululemon pattern in miniature. The tell is this: a brand can be simultaneously right about its product quality, right about its premium positioning, and still facing margin compression if the cost structure is concentrated in a single region and the consumer in the home market is starting to feel price resistance. Lululemon's gross margin in Q1 FY2026 is still 54.2% - structurally excellent by any peer comparison - and the company is still selling premium leggings at $90-$140. The problem is not that the model is broken. The problem is that a model built around vertically-integrated premium pricing, with sourcing concentrated in Asia, got caught by a tariff regime that the FY2023 filing language described as "not expected to have a material impact."

I have seen this pattern in client work where a brand's margin relied on a sourcing configuration that felt permanent until a policy change or supplier disruption made it variable overnight. The early warning is never in the revenue line. It is in the gross margin trend vs. your cost-of-goods-sold geography: specifically, what share of your COGS is exposed to a single tariff jurisdiction, and what happens to your operating income if that jurisdiction's duty rate moves 10-20 points.

**Early-warning scorecard - five lines that catch this 12 months early:**

1.   **Comparable sales direction vs. total revenue growth:** when total revenue is growing but comparable sales are negative, you are opening stores to cover for volume loss per store. Lululemon's total +5% in FY2025 masked Americas comps -3%. If your topline is growing but same-store metrics are declining, the growth is leasing, not compounding.
2.   **Gross margin trend vs. COGS sourcing geography:** the FY2024 filing said tariffs were "not expected to be material." The FY2025 filing quantified $275M of gross profit destruction. The risk was visible in the sourcing concentration - Vietnam, Bangladesh, Cambodia - before the tariff rate moved. Know what percentage of your COGS traces to a single country or tariff bloc.
3.   **Operating margin vs. SG&A absolute dollars:** when revenue growth slows while SG&A grows in absolute terms, operating margin compresses disproportionately. LULU's SG&A grew +8% YoY on a TTM basis through Q1 FY2026 while revenue grew ~4%. That gap is the operating-cost problem bears have correctly identified.
4.   **Single-segment growth coverage ratio:** if one segment (China) is growing fast enough to offset another segment (Americas) declining, your total revenue is stable but your geographic concentration is rising. The right metric is: what share of your revenue growth is coming from a segment that is less than 20% of your revenue base?
5.   **Brand pricing index vs. nearest premium substitute:** track the price gap between your hero SKU and the next-closest competitor. If Alo Yoga's equivalent legging is within $10-15 of yours, the pricing power that justified your gross margin is being competed away. When that gap narrows, the "selective price increase" lever closes before you reach for it.

## Frequently asked questions

is lululemon profitable?
Lululemon remains solidly profitable on an annual basis: FY2025 net income was $1.58B on $11.1B of revenue, and the company holds $1.81B in cash with zero financial debt. However, margin compression is significant - operating margin fell to 19.9% in FY2025 from a peak of 23.7% in FY2024, and Q1 FY2026 operating margin collapsed to 11.2% as tariffs and Americas weakness hit simultaneously.

why is lululemon's stock down so much in 2026?
Three forces converged: (1) Americas comparable sales fell -3% in FY2025, with the decline continuing into Q1 FY2026, signaling structural demand softness not just an assortment issue; (2) the Trump administration's tariffs and de minimis removal destroyed approximately $275M of gross profit in FY2025, with the full run-rate impact showing up in Q1 FY2026's 11.2% operating margin; (3) management acknowledged 'disappointing product launches' in Q1 FY2026, compounding the tariff hit with an execution miss.

what happened to lululemon's gross margin?
Gross margin peaked at 59.2% in FY2024, then fell 260 basis points to 56.6% in FY2025 as tariffs on US-bound goods from Vietnam, Bangladesh and Cambodia hit product costs. The de minimis removal on August 29, 2025 further compressed US e-commerce margin because lululemon historically fulfilled US DTC orders from Canadian distribution centers. Q1 FY2026 gross margin fell to 54.2%, down 410 basis points year-over-year. Management has stated mitigating actions 'are not expected to fully offset these incremental costs.' Source: LULU 10-K FY2025; 10-Q Q1 FY2026.

how big is lululemon's china business?
China Mainland generated $1.75B in revenue in FY2025, up +29% year-over-year, with comparable sales +20% (+19% constant currency). It represents 15.8% of total FY2025 revenue, up from 7.1% in FY2022. China has been the company's fastest-growing segment for three consecutive years, with a 3-year revenue CAGR of approximately 45% from $577M (FY2022) to $1.75B (FY2025). Source: LULU 10-K FY2025.

does lululemon have debt?
No. Lululemon has carried zero financial debt since at least FY2023. As of February 1, 2026 (FY2025 year-end), the company held $1.81B in cash with a $600M revolving credit facility (undrawn, maturing October 2030). The company also repurchased approximately $4.6B of its own shares over the five fiscal years FY2021-FY2025. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025, Notes to Financial Statements.

who are lululemon's main competitors?
The FY2025 10-K names Nike, Adidas, Under Armour, and Athleta (Gap) as direct competitors. Alo Yoga and Vuori are the private-company challengers the risk-factor language increasingly points toward, without naming them: both compete at similar premium price points in women's yoga and men's lifestyle, respectively. Vuori received $400M from SoftBank and has announced plans to open 100+ US stores, making it the most direct structural competitive threat in men's athleisure over the next three years. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025 Item 1A; industry commentary.

what is lululemon's fiscal year?
Lululemon operates a 52/53-week fiscal year ending on the Sunday nearest January 31. FY2025 ended February 1, 2026 (52 weeks). FY2024 ended February 2, 2025 and was a 53-week year. When comparing FY2024 to FY2025, the extra week in FY2024 mildly inflates the FY2024 denominator, making FY2025 growth rates slightly understated on a like-for-like basis. Source: LULU 10-K FY2025.

what was the mirror acquisition and is it still a problem?
Lululemon acquired Mirror Inc. for approximately $500M in 2020, a connected-fitness hardware company. The strategy failed: in FY2022, the company took a $442.7M after-tax impairment charge (goodwill $362.5M plus intangibles and hardware inventory). In FY2023, final wind-down charges added $74.5M after-tax. The digital app pivot (lululemon Studio) was launched in summer 2023 but the risk-factor language largely disappeared by FY2024. Mirror is a resolved event - roughly $1B of capital destroyed (acquisition price ~$500M plus $442.7M impairment plus $74.5M wind-down charges plus operating losses through wind-down), fully written off, with no ongoing financial overhang. Source: LULU 10-K FY2022, FY2023.
