Title: Lululemon - Business Maturity Confused With Brand Decline

URL Source: https://seapointinvestingnotes.substack.com/p/investment-case-for-lululemon-misinterpreting

Published Time: 2026-05-23T13:10:57+00:00

Markdown Content:
The athleisure segment within apparel is unique to most other categories, largely because it is still relatively early in its development.

In more mature segments like footwear and general sportswear, companies such as Nike and Adidas grow primarily through product cycles and incremental demand within an already established global market. Growth is driven more by innovation within existing categories than by structural expansion of the category itself. Athleisure, by contrast, is still evolving more than two decades after Chip Wilson founded Lululemon. I’ll come back to Chip.

When a category generates strong growth and high returns on invested capital, it inevitably attracts new entrants. That is a natural feature of competitive markets. The real question for Lululemon is whether its brand strength and economics remain durable as competition increases. In most industries, rising competition leads to saturation over time and returns gradually converge toward their cost of capital. However, the arrival of new competitors does not automatically imply brand deterioration. That is the source of dislocation between price and fundamental value we have with Lululemon.

Lululemon has experienced a massive sell-off in recent years, down roughly 75% from its post COVID peak in December 2023. This has often been interpreted solely as company specific weakness, but the broader sector suggests a more cyclical and industry wide dynamic. Over the same period, Nike is down around 64%, Adidas down around 24%, and Under Armour down 43%. This points to an unusually strong demand environment during and after COVID, margin pressure from tariffs, and inventory corrections. In contrast, global fast fashion leaders have performed well. Inditex (Zara, Pull&Bear, Bershka) is up 59% and Fast Retailing (Uniqlo) is up 82%. These companies, however, operate in a structurally different segment of the apparel industry. Fast fashion and value oriented retailers compete primarily on speed, assortment breadth, and price accessibility, serving a broader and more price sensitive consumer base. Lululemon, by contrast, sits in the premium performance and lifestyle segment, where demand is driven more by product quality, fit, and brand power.

Lululemon was founded in 1998 by Chip Wilson after he attended a yoga class and noticed how poorly traditional cotton apparel performed. From that observation, the company helped define what would become the athleisure category.

Wilson remains a notable figure in the company’s history and still holds a meaningful stake, roughly 9% of outstanding shares. His departure in 2015 was controversial and followed some public comments he made about the brand’s inclusivity amongst women of different body shapes and sizes. Since then, he has remained a vocal critic of the business and its strategic direction, which has contributed to a persistent narrative overhang on Wall Street. Interestingly, since his departure, revenue has increased roughly 5x and earnings per share around 7x. Regardless of Chip’s narrative, the operating performance has compounded. Still, despite this, the tension has recently escalated, with Wilson launching an activist campaign against the company. You can read it about it [here](https://corporate.lululemon.com/media/press-releases/2026/05-18-2026-123025131).

The focus of this article is not whether the brand requires a turnaround or about the new CEO and what she will mean for the brand going forward. In retail, that’s all too subjective. What matters more is where the business sits in its lifecycle and whether the market is appropriately valuing its underlying brand durability.

There is a tendency to interpret the rapid growth of brands like Alo and Vuori as evidence of superior brand power, while interpreting Lululemon’s slower growth as brand deterioration. In reality, these are companies at very different stages of development. Lululemon operates at a far more mature level of store footprint and brand penetration, particularly in North America, whereas Alo and Vuori are in earlier phases of expansion and building awareness.

Lulu operates a retail model that is meaningfully different from traditional wholesale driven apparel businesses.

In a wholesale model, brands typically sell inventory to third party retailers at a discount, who then handle pricing, merchandising, and the direct customer relationship. This limits visibility into end consumer behaviour and reduces the share of final retail price captured by the brand. Lululemon operates primarily through a direct to consumer structure, where it sells through its own stores and online channels, removing the intermediary layer and giving the company direct control over pricing, inventory, and customer engagement.

Physical stores play an important role in the system, particularly in categories like leggings where fit and product feel matter. They provide a way for customers to evaluate products directly, reducing uncertainty around sizing and quality before purchase. In many cases, the initial store visit is followed by repeat purchasing through digital channels once familiarity with the product is established.

Compared with wholesale models, this approach generally results in higher gross margins, since the company retains the full retail price rather than selling at a discount to intermediaries. It also provides more direct access to customer data and allows for more responsive inventory management.

Over time, this has supported gross margins in the mid-50% range and operating margins around 20%, placing the business at a significantly higher end of apparel economics.

A common mistake in interpreting Lululemon is treating flat same store sales as a signal of brand deterioration. Same store sales are better understood as a function of store maturity rather than a direct measure of brand strength.

The same underlying lifecycle tends to apply across all brands operating this model.

For a new store, the typical progression looks like this:

*   Phase 1 (years 0 to 2): rapid awareness driven growth

*   Phase 2 (years 2 to 5): strong but slowing growth

*   Phase 3 (years 5 to 10): mature repeat purchase phase

*   Phase 4 (10 plus years): saturation and replacement driven stability

What matters is where each store base sits within this cycle.

In Lululemon’s case, North America is largely in Phase 3 to Phase 4. Store density is high and brand awareness is already saturated. At this stage, flat or low growth same store sales are a normal outcome of maturity rather than an indication of weakening brand power.

Emerging competitors such as Alo and Vuori sit much earlier in the cycle. Most of their North American store base is in Phase 1 to Phase 2, with only a smaller portion moving into Phase 3. Globally, they are still predominantly in Phase 1. As a result, their growth rates are structurally higher, but that is a function of store age rather than a difference in brand durability. Over time, these differences naturally compress as stores age through the same lifecycle and growth rates normalise.

The most interesting point will come when newer entrants reach Phase 4 in the US market, where expansion is no longer the driver and performance depends on replacement demand and repeat purchasing. At that point, brand strength becomes much easier to compare on a like for like basis.

North America remains Lululemon’s largest revenue segment, but its relative importance has already begun to decline as international markets scale more rapidly.

3 years ago, North America accounted for roughly 84% of total revenue. Today, that figure is 70%. Importantly, the shift did not come from contraction in the region. North American revenue still grew 37% over the period. So the change instead reflects the differing stages of market maturity. North America is already highly penetrated and relatively mature, while international markets are earlier in their development and therefore growing at a much faster rate.

As China and the Rest of the World continue to account for a larger share of total revenue, overall company growth should gradually increase, at least over the short-medium term. The business becomes less dependent on the already mature North American market and increasingly exposed to regions that are still earlier in their expansion cycle. In that sense, the evolution of geographic exposure becomes an important offset to maturity in North America, rather than a separate or competing driver.

*   No debt

*   Significant buybacks, roughly $2.9 billion over the last two years which is significant on a current market cap of $14.5 billion, though important to note they were done at significantly higher share prices to today

*   10-year average gross margin 56%, last year was 57%

*   10-year average EBIT margin of 21%, last year was 20%

*   10-year average ROIC of 36%, last year 32%

The valuation disconnect becomes difficult to ignore when compared against broader apparel peers.

On almost every operating metric, Lululemon screens as the strongest business in the group. It has the highest EBIT margin, the highest returns on invested capital, a debt free balance sheet, and structurally stronger direct to consumer economics than peers. Yet despite that, it trades at by far the lowest EV/EBIT multiple in the group.

The market appears to be valuing Lululemon as though maturity in North America implies brand deterioration. That is really the core question behind the stock today.

Alo and Vuori are private companies but reported valuations are still interesting. Alo’s parent reportedly targeted a valuation of roughly $10 billion on around $2 billion in revenue, while Vuori’s latest funding round implied a valuation near $5.5 billion on approximately $1 billion in revenue. Both imply revenue multiples around 5x, versus roughly 1.3x for Lululemon.

I would not use them as direct comparables given they are in a completely different phase of their lifecycle. Both are still benefiting from early stage store rollout and awareness driven growth, while Lululemon already operates at scale. As discussed, that distinction matters but shows the market is comparing growth rates between businesses at entirely different stages, and in Lululemon’s case, confusing business maturity with weakening brand power.

*   Retail is structurally difficult to forecast, with performance driven by shifting consumer tastes, fashion cycles, and discretionary spending patterns.

*   For Lululemon, competition risk remains, particularly if newer athleisure brands scale more successfully than expected and sustain cultural relevance beyond early growth phases.

*   New brands like Alo and Vuori will almost certainly continue to grow at a faster rate than Lululemon over the short-medium term, leaving uncertainty around Lululemon’s brand durability.

*   North American stagnation may persist longer than anticipated, limiting near-term growth, while international expansion, particularly in China, carries execution and macro risk.

*   The business is exposed to macroeconomic cycles, with discretionary apparel spending sensitive to employment levels, interest rates, and consumer confidence.

*   Input cost pressure and supply chain disruption remain ongoing risks, including tariffs and broader geopolitical tensions affecting global trade flows.

*   Geopolitical shocks, including disruption to key shipping routes such as the Strait of Hormuz, could indirectly impact freight costs and supply chain stability.

*   There is a valuation risk that the market is correctly pricing in structurally lower growth for apparel, meaning multiple compression could persist even if fundamentals remain strong.

This is a company that’s compounded earnings per share at a staggering 21% since it went public (up 28x). Shareholder returns have compounded at 12% (up 7x). Over nearly two decades, that difference is quite unusual.

Lululemon is no longer a hyper-growth story in its core North American market. That phase is behind it and unlikely to return. What remains is a highly profitable, globally expanding apparel brand with strong unit economics, a direct-to-consumer model with structural advantages, and a leading position in premium athletic apparel.

The market is pricing its transition in North America as if competitive pressure and saturation have permanently impaired the business model. In my view, this reflects a misclassification of lifecycle stage in the area rather than a deterioration in brand quality or cash-generating ability.

Priced at almost a quarter of more mature comparable companies’ valuations, I believe the market is offering a high quality business at a great price.
